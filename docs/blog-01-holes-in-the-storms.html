Holes In The Storms

Why RadEcho punched black gaps through solid precipitation, the twelve attempts that failed to fix it, and the realization that made the problem disappear instead of shrink.

The Blast Radius · 8 August 2026
← The Blast Radius What's New → Open RadEcho →

The symptom

The most persistent defect report against RadEcho has been that storms render chewed up. Black streaks through the middle of solid cells. Pepper holes in a rain shield that has no business having holes in it. Zoomed in on a strong core, the field looks moth-eaten.

For months the working assumption was that this was a tuning problem — that some threshold was slightly wrong and the right constant would close the gaps. That assumption was wrong, and it cost about a dozen failed attempts to establish it. What follows is the whole chain: what the holes actually were, why every obvious fix failed, what finally worked, and how the current build differs structurally from the ones before it.

It gets into the weeds, deliberately.

What the radar actually produces

A WSR-88D transmits a pulse and listens. From the returned signal the site processor computes a set of moments — one number per gate, where a gate is a single range bin along a single beam direction, roughly 250 m deep and one degree wide.

The one everybody knows is reflectivity (dBZ) — how much power came back, loosely how much stuff is up there. Since the 2012 dual-pol upgrade there are also polarimetric moments, and those are where this story lives. The radar transmits and receives on both horizontal and vertical polarization, then compares the two returns:

  • ZDR (differential reflectivity) — is the target wider than it is tall? Large raindrops flatten as they fall, so rain runs slightly positive. Insects run very positive, which turns out to matter later.
  • ρHV (correlation coefficient) — do the H and V returns agree with each other, pulse pair to pulse pair? A volume full of raindrops, all roughly the same shape, gives a very high correlation, 0.98 and up. A jumble of different shapes — bugs, birds, a hillside, tumbling hail — decorrelates, and the number drops.

ρHV looks like a beautiful discriminator. Rain is uniform, junk is not, so junk has low correlation. That framing is what caused the trouble.

The original sin: a threshold

RadEcho's filter was one line of intent: if a gate's ρHV is below 0.95, don't draw it. That is where the holes came from. Every black gap in the display was a gate that had a perfectly good reflectivity reading and got deleted because a second measurement said the scatterers in that volume weren't uniform enough.

There was a reason for it. Early on, the app displayed convincing-looking storms that weren't there — a real problem, particularly on the clear-air scan patterns, where the radar is looking at an empty sky and every bug and inversion layer shows up. The filter was the fix for that, and it worked for that.

The trouble is that thresholding is binary and per-gate, and it has no idea why a given gate's correlation is low.

Why a single threshold can never work

The published operational literature — the same hydrometeor classification work the NWS uses — puts ground clutter and anomalous propagation in a ρHV range of roughly 0.5 to 0.95. It puts heavy rain at roughly 0.92 to 1.0.

Those overlap. Not at the edges — meaningfully. Which means no threshold anywhere on that axis cleanly separates the two populations. Set the cut low and clutter survives; set it high and real rain starts disappearing. No value does both, because the quantity being measured doesn't carry enough information to make the call on its own.

It is the same shape as squelching on signal strength alone. A squelch level can be set to kill the noise, or set to let weak stations through, but where the two overlap in level no setting does both. Separating them requires a second axis — something about the character of the signal rather than its magnitude.

And crucially, real weather drops below 0.95 for reasons that have nothing to do with clutter:

Non-uniform beam filling

The main lobe is about one degree wide. At 200 km that's roughly 3.5 km across. If the storm fills only part of that volume, the sample averages returns from precipitation and empty air together, and the H and V returns decorrelate — not because the rain is unusual, but because one number is being asked to describe a heterogeneous volume. This is the dominant cause of long-range holes, and it worsens with range, exactly where the beam is widest.

The melting layer

Half-melted snowflakes are genuinely irregular — wet, aggregated, tumbling. Correlation legitimately craters. That's the bright band, and it's real weather.

Hail and mixed phase

Tumbling irregular ice in the strongest part of the strongest storms. Low ρHV is the signature of hail, and the filter was treating it as grounds for erasing the core.

So the holes weren't a bug in the implementation. They were what the approach does.

Twelve attempts, and the pattern in the failures

The obvious response is to stop deleting on ρHV alone and add a second condition that recognizes real weather and spares it. That is exactly what was attempted, repeatedly, and the trail is worth reading because the failures rhyme.

  • Spatial extent. Real storms are broad, clutter is point-like. Held up on two cases, then a confirmed anomalous-propagation cluster turned out to be broader than two confirmed real ones.
  • Reflectivity floor. Only rescue gates above some dBZ. Found a live counter-example the same night: three stations reading 45–54 dBZ with zero real precipitation anywhere near them.
  • Whole-population persistence. Clutter sits still, storms move. Broke on the second round of data — a fake station's scattered point sources moved around as much as a real storm.
  • Short-window displacement. Broke immediately.
  • Simple recurrence. Clean for 45 minutes, then a real multi-cell cluster regenerating over the same terrain-favored spot did exactly what clutter does.
  • Occupancy fraction. The best of the lot — one fake station sat at 100% presence over 2.5 hours while real cases topped out near 47%. Then a second fake station scored 53% and an actively flooding real storm scored 100%.
  • Reflectivity texture, then ZDR, then both together. Medians overlapped completely. On one run the fake station had the smoothest texture of all seven.
  • ΦDP texture, after building the plumbing to read a moment the pipeline had never parsed. Real signal on two stations, indistinguishable on a third that shared the same scan pattern.
  • A faithful multi-variable classifier combining the lot. Real stations scored 0.222–0.320. Fake stations scored 0.222–0.333. One fake station scored identically to two real ones.
The pattern: every single one of those was a different statistic computed on the same measurement. Twelve ways of asking ρHV and its neighbors the same question, and getting twelve versions of the same shrug. Each looked clean on two or three cases and broke on the next check — often enough that "clean on n=3" stopped being evidence of anything.

What finally worked

The breakthrough was reading what the WSR-88D fleet actually uses to find clutter, which turns out not to be a hydrometeor classifier at all. It is CMD — Clutter Mitigation Decision — and it works on a completely different axis:

  • TDBZ — how much reflectivity jumps from one gate to the next along a single radial.
  • SPIN — how often it reverses direction, up-down-up-down, over that same run.
  • Plus the standard deviation of ZDR and ΦDP over a short window.

Not "how strong is this return" but "how ragged is it." Rain, even heavy rain, is spatially smooth at 250 m resolution — move one gate over and the value barely changes. A hillside, a tower, a scattering of ground targets produces a signal that slams up and down gate to gate, because the sample is hitting discrete hard objects rather than a medium.

Worth noting what is not in that list: ρHV appears nowhere among CMD's parameters. Twelve rounds of empirical failure, arriving independently at a feature set published in 2009.

One caveat on the resemblance, because it is not a clean match. The version running on the fleet is non-polarimetric, and its third parameter is Clutter Phase Alignment — the pulse-to-pulse phase stability of the time series, computed directly from the I/Q. Ground targets are phase-coherent from pulse to pulse in a way distributed scatterers are not, and CPA is the strongest single discriminator of the three.

RadEcho cannot compute it. CPA needs the time series, which does not survive into a moment file. What RadEcho runs is the documented polarimetric enhancement — the standard deviations of ZDR and ΦDP, worth roughly 3 dB of detection performance according to the same work — bolted onto the two texture terms, with the strongest term of the operational algorithm simply unavailable. That is a fair description of the whole situation: the fleet's algorithm minus what only the site can see.

Two additions made it work in practice. A gate that fails the correlation test gets restored if CMD says it isn't ragged — but only if ZDR is under 3.0 dB, which is the biological ceiling. Insects are wildly oblate; CMD alone happily restored 12–24% of an insect bloom because bugs are spatially smooth. The ZDR ceiling collapses that to well under 1% while keeping 93–97% of the genuine restorations.

And then the realization that made all of it beside the point

Somewhere in the middle of this, an obvious question finally got asked: how does RadarScope handle this?

It doesn't. RadarScope shows Level II reflectivity essentially as the radar produced it, and offers correlation coefficient as a separate product — a layer to be inspected, never a mask that erases anything. WeatherFront's entire reflectivity control is a user-set minimum dBZ, adjustable from −10 to +40, plus a smoothing selector. No dual-pol quality control on reflectivity at all.

Neither of them has this problem because neither of them does this thing. And there's a reason it works out for them, which is the genuinely important piece of physics in this whole story.

The radar already cleaned itself, and it did it properly

The site processor removes ground clutter before reflectivity is ever computed. It does it in the frequency domain, on the raw I/Q: ground doesn't move, so its return sits at essentially zero Doppler, and the processor notches out that spectral component while leaving the weather's spread-out spectrum intact.

It is, functionally, a notch filter at DC — zero frequency, where anything stationary lands. Because it operates on the spectrum, it removes the clutter's contribution to a gate rather than removing the gate. A range bin containing both a hillside and rain comes out the other side with the rain still in it.

That notch is not applied everywhere, and the reason is instructive. Filtering every bin also bites into weather with near-zero radial velocity — a cell moving across the beam rather than along it, or an area of vertical motion inside a storm, puts its energy near DC too. The WSR-88D implementation paper shows exactly this, describing large reflectivity biases in an all-bins-filtered case "due to near zero radial velocities in this region, probably associated with an area of vertical air motion within the cell," and notes that selective filtering "retains more meteorological return as fewer bins are filtered and thus unbiased by the clutter filters."

So the radar has to decide, gate by gate, where filtering is safe. The algorithm that makes that call is CMD — the same one described earlier. It computes a clutter probability for every range bin, and where that probability exceeds 0.5 it raises a clutter flag; the RDA then uses that flag array to select filtered or unfiltered data per gate. It replaced the static bypass maps that preceded it, and went operational with RDA Build 11.0 in the summer of 2009.

Which closes a loop worth noticing. Upstream, CMD decides where the notch may safely be applied. Downstream, RadEcho uses the same score to decide what colour to paint a gate. Same algorithm, opposite ends of the pipeline, doing the only two things each end is capable of.

Worth being exact about what this leaves behind, because it is easy to picture the clutter being stripped out and then folded back in somewhere. It isn't. Where the filter runs, that clutter is permanently gone — the moments in a Level II file are computed from the already-filtered spectrum, so its contribution never reaches any downstream consumer at all.

What remains welded in is whatever the notch could not catch: anything in motion, and any gate where the filter was not applied. That residue is now part of the same single number as the weather sharing its volume, and no downstream processing can split them.

The reason is not re-mixing, it is dimensionality loss. A spectrum is many values across frequency. A moment is a summary of it — total power, mean velocity, spread. Told that five numbers average to 12, the five numbers are not recoverable. Reflectivity is that average: it reports the total power returned by a gate, and nothing in it says how much came from the hillside and how much from the rain. The information needed to make that split existed in the spectrum and was discarded when the moment was computed.

Which leaves exactly one lever downstream: keep the gate, or drop it.

The site processor filters before the measurement exists; RadEcho was thresholding after it. The first is surgical. The second is a hole punch. No amount of cleverness in the second closes that gap, because the information needed to be surgical was discarded upstream.

Everyone receives the same data

RadEcho, RadarScope and WeatherFront all read the same Level II files from the same NOAA bucket, and the site processor's clutter filtering has already been applied to every one of them equally. There is no cleaner internal version being held back — Level II is the product, and the I/Q it was derived from is not retained for anyone.

Level II is routinely described as raw. It is the base moments the RDA signal processor outputs — after clutter filtering, after censoring, after velocity dealiasing. It distinguishes the base data from the derived Level III products, rather than indicating that no processing has occurred.

The asymmetry is not in what arrives, it's in what operations are still available. GMAP subtracts: working on the spectrum, it removes clutter's contribution to a gate and leaves whatever weather shared that volume. Once a moment file exists, the surviving clutter and the weather are one number and that decomposition is unrecoverable. The only operation left is removing the entire gate. Same input; upstream had subtraction, downstream has deletion.

What survives the filter, on purpose

GMAP catches the near-zero-Doppler spectral peak, which is to say it catches things that don't move. Everything else passes through by design, because there is no way to remove moving targets without removing weather along with them:

  • Anything in motion. Turbine blades, birds, insects, wave-driven sea clutter.
  • Ground return where the radar doesn't expect ground. Under ducting, the beam bends into terrain that a normally-propagating beam would clear — and the RDA's sense of where clutter lives is built around normal propagation.
  • Residual from gates where suppression was only partial.

So RadarScope and WeatherFront are not displaying cleaner data. They are displaying the same residue and relying on the operator to recognise it — which, for their audience, is a reasonable bet.

Given identical data, the reason RadEcho added a filter was the original defect report: convincing fake storms on the clear-air scan patterns. That population — biological scatter and anomalous propagation — is precisely what GMAP leaves behind, so the filter was aimed at a real gap. It used an instrument that could not distinguish that gap from heavy rain.

So RadEcho stopped deleting things

The current build does not remove non-weather from the default view. It labels it.

Rendered asWhat it means
Slate greyBiological — insects, birds. Tagged by ZDR at or above 3.0 dB.
Earthy brownGround clutter or anomalous propagation. Failed the correlation test and scored ragged on CMD.
Normal dBZ rampEverything else, including gates that failed the correlation test but read as smooth.

The distinction that matters:

The holes are gone by construction, not by tuning. There is no threshold left in the default path that can delete a gate. Nothing to get slightly wrong, no constant that has to be right for a storm to render intact. The gaps didn't get smaller — the mechanism that produced them is no longer in the picture.

A second-order point matters just as much. Deleting hides its own mistakes. If a classifier is wrong some fraction of the time and its response is to erase the gate, the evidence is destroyed and nobody ever learns. Colouring it means a wrong call costs a wrong colour — the echo is still there, still positioned, still arguable. The classifier reports on itself, in production, in the open.

Put another way: absence is the one error that cannot be audited. A false echo can be examined and discounted. A hole asserts "there is nothing here," which is a factual claim, and when it is wrong there is nothing left to notice.

How this compares to the other apps

The most useful distinction between radar apps is not the feature list. It is whether the display is single-site — one radar's own view, at one elevation angle, at one timestamp — or a national composite like MRMS, which merges every radar in the network onto a common grid and applies its own quality control on the way.

Composites look cleaner, and there is a real reason for it beyond polish: MRMS can cross-check one radar against its neighbours. A wind farm visible to one radar is invisible to another looking at the same volume from a different angle and a different height, so it can be voted down. That is genuinely independent evidence, and no single-site display has access to it.

The cost is resolution and latency. A composite is resampled onto a shared grid and blended across sources, so the fine structure of one radar's native 250 m gates is not what reaches the screen.

Read across the middle rows and the single-site apps agree with each other: none of them use dual-pol to erase reflectivity. RadEcho was the outlier in doing so, and the current build brings it back in line — with the addition that non-weather is marked rather than merely present.

The comparison against a composite is different in kind. A phone weather app showing MRMS will not display the Fredonia turbines, because a neighbouring radar contradicted them. RadEcho will, because it has one point of view and reports what that view contains. Whether that is a limitation or the point depends on what the display is for: a composite answers "is it raining on me," while a single-site view answers "what is this radar actually seeing, and how sure is it."

Known problems in the current build

All of these are visible in the current build, and all are open items.

There is a wind farm rendering as a severe thunderstorm

At 42.4154, −79.2283 — the escarpment above Fredonia, New York, about 72 km from the Buffalo radar — there is a persistent 50 dBZ return that never moves. Over 15 consecutive volumes spanning 90 minutes, of 3,576 gates that touched 45 dBZ at least once, exactly one held it in every single volume. That one.

It's almost certainly the Arkwright Summit turbine array, and the reason it's there is a genuinely elegant failure:

Turbine blades move. The site processor's clutter filter works by notching out near-zero Doppler. Spinning blades aren't near-zero Doppler, so they sail straight through the one filter specifically designed to catch ground returns. Then they read ρHV = 0.985, which clears the threshold comfortably, so they never even reach the clutter classifier.

Nothing in the current build catches this. Not the correlation test, not CMD, not the height guard below. It renders as weather in every view. Wind turbine clutter is a known, documented, unsolved problem across the operational community — forecasters are trained to recognize it rather than expect it removed.

Terrain that lights up when a storm goes over it

Southeast of Fredonia, the Chautauqua escarpment rises hard off the lake plain. It produces a persistent brown patch — correctly labelled, it really is ground. What's interesting is that the patch grows noticeably when a storm passes overhead.

That isn't the hills changing. A thunderstorm's cold pool spreads a layer of cool, moist air near the surface, which creates a refractive gradient that bends the beam downward. Terrain the beam normally passes over gets clipped, and the return jumps.

This is tropospheric ducting — the same superrefraction behind improbable VHF and UHF DX on a humid summer evening. Desirable in one context, a nuisance in this one. The geometry here is unusually marginal: at 72 km the beam bottom sits around 349 m above the radar, and the escarpment tops out around 340–380 m above the same reference. It is grazing the ridge, and a fraction of a degree of extra bending is the difference between passing over and hitting.

Deep convection at long range sometimes gets called clutter

CMD scores raggedness, and hail is genuinely ragged. A hail-bearing core 200+ km out, where the beam is several kilometres wide and only partly filled, produces exactly the texture signature CMD was built to flag. So brown streaks occasionally appear through real storm cores at extreme range.

One case of this got a targeted fix worth mentioning, because the bug is funny in hindsight. CMD is a pure texture calculation — it has no idea how far away a gate is. So it was cheerfully labelling returns "ground clutter" at ranges where, thanks to earth curvature, the beam is kilometres above any ground: 

Bracebridge, Ontario — 237 km from KBUF, 0.5° tilt

  beam centre = 237 km · sin(0.5°)  +  237² / (2 · 8500)
              = 2,064 m  +  3,290 m   =  5,354 m above the radar
  beam width  ≈ 3.8 km at that range
  → sampled volume spans roughly 3.7 – 7.5 km above sea level

The earth has curved away by more than three kilometres beneath the beam. There is no ground in that volume. So the current build refuses to apply the ground-clutter label above 3 km above radar level — which, at the lowest tilt, kicks in past about 165–185 km depending on the exact elevation angle. Below that, nothing changed at all: on the verification volume, clutter labels inside 160 km were identical before and after (15,222 gates), while beyond 160 km they dropped from 1,043 to 98.

That residual 98 is real ground clutter at range, correctly labelled. The ~945 that went away were storms being called dirt.

What the current build does and does not guarantee

For the specific defect — holes through storms — the result comes from structure rather than tuning. The default view has no delete step at all. The threshold that produced the holes still exists in the codebase, but only runs for someone who deliberately opts into it via the control beside the legend.

Concretely, on a real Albany volume from the afternoon this shipped: 

Hide low-confidence returns ON  (the old behaviour)
    grey  13,316 px      brown      0 px

Hide low-confidence returns OFF (the default now)
    grey  15,499 px      brown  7,447 px

Nothing was thrown away in the second case. Roughly 7,400 pixels' worth of ground clutter that used to be deleted is now visible and marked, and about 2,200 pixels of biological return that used to be deleted came back as well.

The classification is not right every time — see the wind farm above. What the build guarantees is narrower:

Nothing is removed from the display, and every return carries a best guess at what it is. Not that everything on screen is precipitation.

One more caveat on the colours: they're placeholders. The grey was reverse-engineered to match what the previous translucent version composited to on the dark basemap, and the brown was picked to sit outside the dBZ ramp. Neither has had a proper design pass, and on the terrain basemap the brown is genuinely hard to see against tan ground. That's a known open item, not an oversight.

On forecasting, since it keeps coming up

The comparison that gets raised most is Tomorrow.io, and it's worth being clear about what kind of thing each of these actually is, because they're not competing products.

RadEcho displays a measurement. Every pixel corresponds to a specific range bin, at a specific elevation angle, at a specific timestamp, with the raw moment values behind it. Nothing is predicted, extrapolated, or modelled.

Tomorrow.io is a forecast company. Numerical weather prediction, proprietary assimilation, a proprietary sensor network. That is an entirely different engineering problem — model infrastructure rather than display infrastructure, and not something that bolts onto a radar viewer.

Between the two sits nowcasting — 0 to 2 hour extrapolation from radar alone. Echo motion is tracked across consecutive volumes, a displacement field derived, and the current field advected forward. No atmospheric model, no assimilation: this is moving in this direction at this speed, so here is where it lands in 40 minutes.

That is tractable here, and some of the groundwork already exists — the frame-to-frame pairing needed to track features was built and verified for an unrelated experiment. The output would be storm-relative arrival estimates for a saved location — a more honest product than a general forecast, because it is an extrapolation of a measurement and degrades gracefully: the thing being extrapolated from can be shown alongside it.

What advection can never do is anticipate a storm that has not formed yet. It can only move what already exists. Convective initiation requires a model, which places it outside what a radar viewer can do from measurements alone.

What's next

  • A terrain layer derived from the radar itself. Persistent ground clutter is, functionally, a map of exactly which ridges this radar's beam intersects. Accumulate the clutter classification over many clear-air volumes and the result is a stable terrain mask — not from a DEM, but the beam's own point of view. Useful for the observation that started this line of thinking: storms weakening as they cross the Catskills' eastern wall, with nothing on screen to explain why.
  • An echo inspector. Hover or tap a return and get what it actually is: persistence across recent frames, motion, ZDR and ρHV, beam height above ground at that range, and the nearest known obstruction from the public FAA and USGS databases. The wind farm above becomes a one-line answer instead of a mystery.
  • Cross-station coherence, carefully scoped. If two radars can see the same volume from different angles, disagreement is real evidence — the first genuinely independent measurement in this whole saga. It turns out to be useless for the wind farm (no neighbouring radar's beam gets within 2 km of those turbines), but it's well suited to anomalous propagation, which was the original complaint that built the filter in the first place.
