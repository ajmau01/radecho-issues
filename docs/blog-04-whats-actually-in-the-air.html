What's Actually In The Air

RadEcho's new air-quality layer: a real-time AQI badge, a translucent band overlay backed by the same EPA polygon service AirNow.gov itself renders from, a clickable layer of every official monitor in view, and click-anywhere crosshair placement that ships alongside all of it.

What's new

RadEcho has always shown what's falling. This is the first layer that shows what's in the air instead — the EPA Air Quality Index, sourced from the same public infrastructure behind AirNow.gov, so a hazy or smoky day carries the same kind of context a storm does. Four pieces ship together: a badge at the crosshair, a translucent band overlay on the map, a layer of clickable individual monitor stations, and a click-anywhere gesture for moving the crosshair itself. All are covered below.

The badge and the overlay

Move the crosshair anywhere in the U.S. and a small pill badge reports the EPA category at that exact point — color-coded to the same six-tier scale AirNow itself uses:

AQI rangeCategoryColor
0–50GoodGreen
51–100ModerateYellow
101–150Unhealthy for Sensitive GroupsOrange
151–200UnhealthyRed
201–300Very UnhealthyPurple
301+HazardousMaroon

The number itself is a combined index over PM2.5 (fine particulates — smoke, haze, combustion byproducts) and ground-level ozone, whichever of the two is currently worse at that location; EPA's own methodology always reports the higher of the two sub-indices as the headline number, since that's the pollutant actually driving health risk at that moment.

Turn on the optional map overlay and the same categories paint as translucent bands across the visible area — a regional picture of where the air is worse, not just a single point. It's backed by a live public EPA/AirNow ArcGIS feature service (AirNow24HrCombined) — the literal interpolated contour surface AirNow.gov's own map renders from, not a synthetic grid. Real polygon geometry, filled directly on the map canvas; a real 24-hour rolling history behind it, so animating back through older radar frames pulls the correct historical hour's air-quality band rather than always showing the current one.

A point reading and a regional band can legitimately disagree near a category boundary — a station's exact spot might sit just inside a Good polygon while a Moderate one starts a mile away. The badge only shows a specific number when it agrees with the category actually painted on the map; otherwise it shows the category alone, so the two never visibly contradict each other on screen.

The station layer

Toggle on Show Station Readings and every active official AirNow monitor currently in view appears as a small dot, colored by its own live category. Click one for a popup with its name, exact reading, what kind of monitor it is (permanent or temporary), and how old the reading is. This is real point data rather than the interpolated regional surface — useful specifically because the two don't always agree near a monitor, and because a contour band can't tell you which physical sensor is actually behind a given reading. The layer only appears on the current/live frame; monitor readings aren't retained with the kind of history the contour service has, so there's nothing honest to show on an older animation frame.

Click anywhere to move the crosshair

A plain click anywhere on the radar map now relocates the crosshair to that exact point — the "Center on" field updates with the new coordinates, the marker redraws there immediately, and the AQI badge re-queries for the new spot, all without leaving the current view or station. It's a genuine relocation, not just a look-around: pan and double-click-zoom still move the view without disturbing a placed crosshair, but a direct click is treated as "put it here." Useful for checking air quality or the radar picture at a specific spot — a neighboring town, a trailhead, wherever — without typing coordinates by hand.

Being honest about where the data comes from

Not every location has an official monitor nearby. Where the coverage gap search comes up empty, the badge falls back to a coarser regional model estimate rather than showing nothing — and it says so explicitly, labeling a real station reading and a modeled estimate differently rather than presenting both the same way. A modeled estimate is a genuinely different kind of number: useful for a general background sense of air quality, not a substitute for a real sensor reading during a fast-moving, localized event like a nearby wildfire.

Historical animation frames never invent a number. If the EPA service hasn't published data for a given hour yet, the badge and overlay show category-only or nothing at all, rather than a fabricated placeholder — the same "label, don't guess" principle the radar rendering itself already follows.

The contour and station services are CONUS-only — Hawaii, Alaska, and Puerto Rico currently show no map overlay, falling back gracefully to whatever point data is available. And the underlying EPA product itself only updates hourly, so within a single radar animation loop (typically 30–40 minutes) the air-quality picture will mostly look static — that's the real temporal resolution of the source data, not a rendering shortcut.

Shipped fixes worth knowing about

  • Badge race condition. Rapid clicking near a category boundary could occasionally settle on a stale, wrong reading if an older network response landed after a newer one. Fixed with a generation counter that discards any response older than the most recent request.
  • Missing from exports. The AQI overlay wasn't included in Save Image / Copy Image / animated GIF exports. Now composited into all three at its correct on-screen position and opacity.
  • Station-dot clicks no longer relocate the crosshair. Clicking a station dot to see its popup was, briefly, also capable of moving the crosshair underneath it — a side effect of two different click handlers both listening on the same area. Fixed so a dot click only ever opens its popup.

What ships off by default

Both the AQI band overlay and the station-dots layer ship unchecked — the standing rule for anything outside the radar itself. The badge at the crosshair is the only piece that's always on.

What's next

A third monitor type — low-cost PurpleAir sensors, which fill real gaps between official stations, especially useful near fast-moving events like wildfire smoke — is the planned next addition to the station layer. It requires a personal, free PurpleAir API key rather than anything the app can provision on its own.
